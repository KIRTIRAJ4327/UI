AI-driven software delivery: specifications or governance?

Two ways to put AI agents to work on real software changes, what each one does, who sells it, and how to choose. Written for technology and business leaders in regulated organisations.

Summary

AI now writes code faster than organisations can review, approve and release it. The two approaches in this report attack that problem from opposite ends, and most large organisations will need both.

1

Spec-driven toolkits control what the agent is asked to build. The team writes the requirement, plan and tasks before any code. The leading toolkits are free, open source and mature: GitHub Spec Kit is at version 1.4 and AWS AI-DLC at 2.11. Read in source

2

Governed delivery platforms control who approves the work and what is released. Every large delivery vendor shipped or announced one in 2026: GitHub, GitLab, Atlassian, ServiceNow, Harness, IBM and Thoughtworks. Vendor claim

3

The two are converging. Toolkits are adding approval gates and audit trails. Platforms are adding planning and specification agents. Read in source Vendor claim

4

Neither has independent proof of better outcomes yet. The largest study of written specifications found no effect on defects. Platform results are vendor-reported. Published summary

5

Our advice: adopt a toolkit now, put governance in the system of record you already own, and build custom only for what vendors do not cover. Today that gap is the experience for business approvers and one evidence record across tools. Our judgement

Section 2

The market in three layers

AI-driven software delivery is easier to understand as three layers. Each layer depends on the one below it. This report compares layers 2 and 3.

  • Layer 1 is a settled, crowded market. Gartner's first Magic Quadrant for Enterprise AI Coding Agents (May 2026) names Anthropic, Cursor, GitHub and OpenAI as Leaders, with AWS, Google, Alibaba Cloud and Cognition as Challengers. Published summary
  • Layer 2 is open source and moving fast. All four toolkits we read had commits in the two days before this report. Read in source
  • Layer 3 is where the large vendors are now competing. Each one extends the system of record it already owns: the repository, the work tracker, the service desk or the pipeline. Our judgement
Section 3

Approach A: spec-driven toolkits

In spec-driven development the team writes down what to build and why before the agent writes code. The requirement, the plan and the task list are text files kept in the repository beside the code. The agent is told to work from those files and nothing else.

What it does well

  • Free and available now. No procurement.
  • No lock-in. Works with almost any coding agent.
  • Fits how developers work. Everything is in the repository and versioned by Git.
  • Adaptable. Presets, extensions and workflows let a team shape the process.
  • Community add-ons fill gaps. Multi-repository dispatch, Azure DevOps with human sign-off, Jira and Linear sync all exist.

Read in source

Where it falls short

  • Built for developers only. No screen for a product owner, release manager or auditor.
  • Approval has no identity in the core. A gate is a prompt in a terminal. AWS AI-DLC's gates are "not assigned to any specific role", one reviewer notes.
  • Documents drift from code. Spec Kit's core has "no dedicated command" to reconcile them.
  • Add-ons are unvetted. Maintainers "do not review, audit, endorse, or support" community code.
  • Stops before release. Deployment and rollback are left to other tools.
  • Heavy for small changes. Thoughtworks' Technology Radar reports "verbose markdown" and "instruction bloat", and rates Spec Kit "Assess", not "Adopt".

Read in source for the quoted Spec Kit text; Published summary for the reviewer and Thoughtworks.

Section 4

Approach B: governed delivery platforms

A governed delivery platform treats an AI agent like a new member of staff inside an existing control framework. The agent may do the work, but a named person approves each important step, the approval is recorded, and release is controlled by policy.

A reference design

To make the category concrete we use a governed delivery console that we designed and prototyped. It is a design, not a product. It shows the full pattern in one place.

Section 6

Reasoning: how to think about the choice

1

They are different kinds of control, so they do not substitute for each other. A specification is a preventive control: it reduces the chance the wrong thing is built. An approval record is an authorising control: it proves a responsible person accepted the result. Auditors and regulators ask for the second. Engineers benefit most from the first.

2

The bottleneck is review, and only one approach addresses it directly. Toolkits add documents to review. Platforms can route, batch and risk-tier approvals. Harness, for example, triggers approval by policy and impact. A platform that applies every gate to every change will make the bottleneck worse.

3

The layers are merging. AWS AI-DLC already has gates, an audit trail and an "Enterprise" profile. A Spec Kit add-on has named sign-offs tied to a fingerprint of the signed files. From the other side, GitLab and Thoughtworks have planning and specification agents. Within one or two years the large vendors are likely to offer both layers together.

4

That changes the case for building your own. A custom governed platform now competes with the roadmaps of GitHub, GitLab, Atlassian and ServiceNow. It is worth building only where they are weak: a clear experience for business approvers, one evidence record across several tools, and explanations of what the agent did.

5

Regulation raises the floor but has not set a deadline for most of this. The European Union has postponed high-risk AI obligations to December 2027. Existing rules on change management and separation of duties already apply to AI-written changes, whoever wrote them.

Section 7

What to do

1

Adopt a spec-driven toolkit in engineering now. Start with one team and one toolkit. Use a light profile for small changes.

2

Turn on the agent governance your current vendors already ship. Ask each one for agent identity, audit logging, approval policy and a stop control before buying anything new.

3

Build custom only for the gap. Today that is the business approver's experience and a single evidence record across tools. Connect to toolkits and platforms; do not replace them.

4

Measure review time and failed changes, not lines of code. Take a baseline before the pilot. Without one, no claim of benefit can be tested.

5

Tier approvals by risk from the first day. Reserve full sign-off for changes that need it.

6

Treat community add-ons as third-party software. Review the code, pin the version, and host approved packages in a private catalog.

Our judgement

Method, limits and sources

  • Source reading. We cloned seven public repositories on 13 November 2026 and read their documentation, catalogs and templates. We did not install or run them, so behaviour is as documented, not as observed.
  • Published summaries. Studies, surveys and product news come from search summaries of the named sources. We could not open the original pages. Check any figure before quoting it outside your organisation.
  • Scores. The matrix and positioning chart are our judgement. Two analysts could reasonably differ by one point on any cell. Cells marked "?" had too little public evidence to score.
  • Not covered. Pricing, security certification, and hands-on product testing. Google's agent products are omitted because reports about their status conflict.
  • Conflicts we noticed. Sources disagree on whether Gartner named three or four Leaders, on Kiro's general-availability date, and on the Harvard paper's approval-time figure.
